Milkless Milk Truck Detection

Most Terrible Problem

Over 7 years ago I took a picture of a milk truck. It brought me great joy and I would frequently look back at it in wonder. Then I misplaced it.

The most obvious solution to this problem is, of course, not to search my whole hard drive to find my most treasured picture, but rather build an object detector model which will then be able to detect a milk truck in a picture, after which I can let an automated script search my whole hard drive while I watch anime.

There is a slight problem, though -- I don't exactly have any images of a milk truck such as that one to train it on.

Synthetic Milk

As I do have the most excellent Blender skills in my possesion, I created a simple replica to the best of my ability -- it was very hard to remember how the truck looked like, exactly.

Fig. 1. Milk Trucks
This took about 30 minutes to create, the model has a very low polygon count as is very rough, but it does get the basic shape across. All in all this is not the most realistic 3D model, but maybe it will be enough to find what we're looking for.

I plopped these trucks in a simple countryside scene. Ideally we would use a multitude of different backgrounds, but alas, a tremendous feeling of slothfulness overcame me and I couldn't be bothered to complete a most arduous task such as the creation of more backgrounds.

Actually Synthesizing The Milk

To train an object detection model, we'll need a lot of images themselves, as well as the tricky bits -- labels, which are the definitions of bounding boxes of objects in the image.

I found the easiest way to create these labels is to render a normal color image and then tweak the 3D model's rendering settings to render the whole model in a magenta sentinel color, thereby creating a segmentation map, after which this image could be processed so as to find the highest/lowest and leftest/rightest magent pixels to use those values for the bounding box.

Fig. 2. Milk Truck Segmentation
Color image. Segment image. Bounding box derived from a segment image. This one is for a different angle, as I misplaced the bounding box image for the other images.

The Milk Trucks were placed in the scene by hand, as there were not that many, but other parameters of the renderings were randomly chosen:

  1. Lighting.

    I chose 3 vaguely realistic looking combinations of ambient color, direct color and their brightnesses, representing dusk, overcast day and a sunny day. The color values get randomly blended together from the 3 presets and get a random color offset added on top.

  2. View position.

    The view position was determined by generating a random normal vector, multiplying its magnitude by a random number and then adding it to a position of a milk truck.

    Additionally a ray-cast is performed from the new view position, to the model of the milk truck, so as to ensure that there will be no scenery blocking the view.

    This also effectively solves the problem of the completely random view offset from being inadvertently placed inside the ground.

  3. View rotation.

    We simply rotate the view so that it points directly at the milk truck, after which we add a random rotation to it, so that the milk truck doesn't end up in the center of all images.

Writing the C++ for this was pretty easy and truly joyous, as I used my most wonderful creation, the Tramway SDK for all of the synthesis tasks.

Fig. 3. Randomly Pictured Milk Trucks

To further augment the dataset, I decided to take the 256x256 synthesized images, crop them to 224x224 images with a random offset, as well as apply various filters:

  1. No filter. The image is cropped and then output as-is.
  2. Horizontal Flip. The image is flipped horizontally.
  3. HSV Shift. The colors of the image are shifted randomly.
  4. Warp. The image is distorted with a sine function.
  5. Skew. The image is randomly sheared.
Fig. 4. Randomly Processed Milk Trucks
Original. Flipped. HSV shifted. Warped. Skewed.

I then tasked my cyberslave thankless servant Claude with the creation Python scripts to do all of the data processing outside of the graphics engine (it's too easy for me to be any fun).

Training The Model

Usually to get good results for a proper object detection model, you need a ton of data. I only have a couple of kilograms, so instead I decided to use a pre-trained model and then only fine-tune it.

I grabbed a relatively small YOLO object detection model and after 2 hours of training (it was done on a 15 year old laptop; don't ask) I finally had a model capable of recognizing a milk truck... or so I thought.

Fig. 5. Flying Over Milk Trucks
Notice how we sometimes get detections when there are no milk trucks on the screen.

The dataset that I used to fine-tune the model had two notable flaws -- the first being the fact that we did not use any non milk-truck examples and the second being the fact that we had no pure background images.

The latter flaw manifested itself most clearly when I ran the model over images being captured live and I noticed that we sometimes get detections when there are no milk trucks on the screen (see Fig. 5.), altough everything works really well when there at least a single milk truck visible, similar to the training data.

Fig. 6. Milk-Fuel Truck Confusion
Fuel truck. Milk truck.

The former flaw was most noticeable when comparing a real image of a fuel truck to an image of an actual milk truck (see Fig. 6.). The YOLO model that I am fine-tuning was trained on the COCO dataset, which contains a lot of examples of trucks, so it seems like we might have taught the model to identify all trucks as milk trucks, which was to be expected.

At least we are making some progress. Had I not known what a truck is and I couldn't appropriately use the pre-trained category, the images of the objects I synthesized were enough to train the model to be capable of filtering out the vast majority of images on my disk drive.

Fig. 7. Additional Negative Examples

To get the model to classify milk trucks specifically with a higher confidence score than other types of trucks, as well as to rectify the issue of detections in images with no trucks whatsoever, I supplemented the dataset with random non-milk truck images procured from Google Images and various non-truck images from my meme and Hatsune Miku fanart folders.

Results and Conclusions

Fig. 8. We Found It!
It turns out that it was not a milk truck at all, but rather a milk trailer... I guess I need to eat more curd so that I have better memory.

At first I didn't even think that this would work at all -- I totally knew that I could get the bounding boxes all generated, maybe even get the model to unreliably work on synthetic data.

I basically created the worst case synthetic data scenario -- very unrealistic, low-poly graphics that look like they belong in a 20 year old video game, augmented with very little real data, all used to fine-tune a relatively tiny model.

But in the end we did manage to locate my most beloved picture.

What's Next?

Hardware upgrade, then I'll start working on algorithms for processing gaussian splats. The idea is to create something that can break apart a scanned 3D scene, then rearrange the parts to create new scenes. Then we use the new scenes for training models.

This should allow us to bridge the gap from synthetic to real data way more easily -- the results we got in this experiment were surprisingly not totally terrible, but there is plenty of room for improvement.
